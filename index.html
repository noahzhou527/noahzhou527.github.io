1. Motivation & Research

The why, the gap, and the stakeholders

This module combines the project motivation, research gap, and stakeholder/persona definition into one evidence-led foundation for Rockbond.

The Why

Why we chose the Active Lifestyles / Go Climbers track

We chose the Active Lifestyles / Go Climbers track because climbing is not only a form of exercise, but also a social and motivational experience. For many student climbers, especially beginners, climbing sits between fitness, leisure, self-improvement, and belonging. Each session gives people visible challenges to attempt, routes to remember, and small achievements to recognise. This makes climbing a strong human-centred design context because progress is personal, emotional, and often shared with others.

At the same time, beginner-to-intermediate climbers often experience gaps between sessions. They may forget routes, lose track of progress, struggle to find partners, or feel unsure whether they are improving. Existing tools can support parts of this experience, but they often separate logging, discovery, community, and motivation. Rockbond responds to this opportunity by proposing a mobile-first climbing companion that helps users record sessions, revisit saved projects, notice progress, discover events, and stay connected through lightweight, supportive community features.

Because the track asks us to design for active lifestyles, it lets us address barriers that affect real participation: confidence, routine, access to companions, and the feeling that effort is meaningful. This focus gives the project a practical health angle while leaving space for playful interaction and community value.

Active lifestyles and climbing

The Gap

Academic evidence and commercial product gaps

The gap review compares six academic papers and four commercial products through three strengths and three missed opportunities, showing what existing research and tools support well and where Rockbond can still add value.

Academic Literature Review

Motivation, self-efficacy, and inclusive engagement

The literature shows that climbing participation is linked to motivation, leisure satisfaction, and physical self-efficacy. Digital support should therefore help users feel that participation is worthwhile, not just record isolated sessions.

Fitness-app research also suggests that social features and gamification can increase engagement, but comparison-based systems need careful framing so they remain supportive rather than discouraging.

1. Lee, Kim, Lee, & Park (2024)Effect of Participation Motivation in Sports Climbing on Leisure Satisfaction and Physical Self-Efficacy

3 things they did well

  1. Directly studied sports climbing motivation rather than general fitness motivation.
  2. Connected motivation with leisure satisfaction and self-efficacy, giving psychological evidence for continued participation.
  3. Showed that climbing motivation is multidimensional, not only competitive or performance-driven.

3 things they missed

  1. Focused more on psychological relationships than digital product or app design.
  2. Did not examine app-mediated/community-supported climbing experiences.
  3. Provided limited insight into long-term social/community interaction patterns.
2. Carter & Allured (2022)Outdoor participation and intent among indoor climbers: findings from the U.S. and Canada

3 things they did well

  1. Focused on indoor climbers, which closely matches a student and gym-based climbing audience.
  2. Showed that indoor-only climbers differ in how central climbing is to their lifestyle and routines.
  3. Highlighted different participation pathways instead of treating all climbers as a single user group.

3 things they missed

  1. Mainly focused on indoor-outdoor transition rather than digital support systems.
  2. Paid limited attention to social app/community interaction features.
  3. Was not campus-specific and therefore less tailored to university climbing environments.
3. Wigfield et al. (2024)A Cross-Sectional, Survey-Based Study of Equity, Diversity, and Inclusion in the Canadian Indoor Climbing Community

3 things they did well

  1. Introduced an EDI (Equity, Diversity, and Inclusion) perspective into climbing research.
  2. Identified social motivations as especially important for some climbing groups.
  3. Highlighted participation constraints and barriers, not only motivations or performance.

3 things they missed

  1. Focused mainly on the Canadian geographic context, limiting broader applicability.
  2. Was not an HCI/app design paper.
  3. Did not translate findings into concrete interface or interaction design decisions.
4. Mangan, Andrews, Miles, & Draper (2025)The psychology of rock climbing: A systematic review

3 things they did well

  1. Provided a broad synthesis across motivation, confidence, anxiety, perception, and decision-making in climbing.
  2. Offered strong high-level grounding for understanding climbing psychology.
  3. Helped justify a more holistic climbing experience beyond isolated performance metrics.

3 things they missed

  1. Focused on broad psychology rather than app/product design.
  2. May have combined very different climbing contexts into one overall discussion.
  3. Did not specify concrete digital product or feature recommendations.
5. Mazeas, Duclos, Pereira, & Chalabaev (2022)Evaluating the Effectiveness of Gamification on Physical Activity: Systematic Review and Meta-analysis

3 things they did well

  1. Provided strong evidence supporting gamification in physical activity contexts.
  2. Used systematic review and meta-analysis methods instead of anecdotal observations.
  3. Offered useful academic justification for playful and motivational design features.

3 things they missed

  1. Was not specifically focused on climbing activities or climbing communities.
  2. Showed that gamification effectiveness varies depending on context and users.
  3. Did not identify which specific mechanics work best for climbing environments.
6. Sun & Jiang (2022)Linking social features of fitness apps with physical activity among Chinese users: Evidence from self-reported and self-tracked behavioral data

3 things they did well

  1. Connected social app features with both self-reported and tracked physical activity behavior.
  2. Demonstrated how social support and social comparison can influence exercise intention.
  3. Provided strong relevance for mobile fitness interaction and community feature design.

3 things they missed

  1. Was not climbing-specific.
  2. Focused on a Chinese fitness app context rather than university climbing communities.
  3. Showed that social comparison may motivate users unevenly and potentially create pressure.
Commercial Product Review

Existing products solve parts of the problem

Climbing-specific apps such as KAYA and Vertical-Life are useful for logging, discovery, and climbing content. ClimbTime supports local gym coordination, while Strava is strong in challenges and social visibility.

However, these products rarely combine lightweight student-friendly progress tracking, climbing-native social connection, and playful motivation in one coherent mobile-first experience.

1. KAYA ClimbClimbing-native route discovery, progress tracking, and community beta sharing

Visit KAYA Climb

3 things it did well

  1. Delivered strong climbing-specific content and a climbing-native community experience.
  2. Combined route discovery, progress tracking, and community interaction in one platform.
  3. Built a rich user-generated beta and sharing ecosystem.

3 things it missed

  1. Focused more on outdoor climbing and beta culture than campus/community onboarding.
  2. Could feel content-heavy or overwhelming for beginners.
  3. Provided limited lightweight motivational onboarding for casual or student users.
2. Vertical-LifeClimbing logging, gym engagement, challenges, and achievement mechanics

Visit Vertical-Life

3 things it did well

  1. Integrated climbing logging with challenge and achievement mechanics.
  2. Supported gym engagement through route logging and progression systems.
  3. Framed climbing progress and milestones clearly for users.

3 things it missed

  1. Challenge-oriented framing may mainly appeal to more committed climbers.
  2. Offered less explicit support for social entry, partner-finding, or beginner confidence-building.
  3. Felt feature-rich but not necessarily emotionally supportive or welcoming for novice users.
3. ClimbTimeLocal climbing community coordination, gym tools, clubs, and events

3 things it did well

  1. Strongly supported local climbing community coordination and participation.
  2. Combined practical gym tools with social connection features.
  3. Helped users connect around real climbing sessions, clubs, and events.

3 things it missed

  1. Was closely tied to gym operations and software ecosystems.
  2. Had less playful or emotionally engaging experience design.
  3. Relied partly on gym-level adoption rather than fully user-driven interaction.
4. Strava Challenges / Group ChallengesGoal-based activity motivation, badges, and social challenge loops

Visit Strava Support

3 things it did well

  1. Used strong motivational framing through goals, badges, and achievement systems.
  2. Made social progress visible within groups and challenge participation.
  3. Created clear challenge loops that encouraged repeated engagement.

3 things it missed

  1. Was not designed specifically for climbing activities or climbing progression.
  2. Could over-emphasize competition, metrics, and performance pressure.
  3. Did not support climbing-specific needs such as gym discovery, route memory, or beta sharing.
Gap Synthesis

A coherent system for everyday climbers is still missing

The academic and commercial review point to the same unmet need: climbers benefit from motivation, self-efficacy, social support, and meaningful progress visibility, but current tools often separate these needs.

Rockbond addresses this gap by focusing on gradual progression, social belonging, and playful motivation for everyday beginner-to-intermediate climbers rather than elite performance alone.

The Stakeholders

Primary and secondary users with distinct personas

Rockbond is shaped around primary users who directly participate in climbing and secondary users who support learning, gym access, events, and community culture.

Stakeholder Identification

The stakeholder groups are divided into primary users who directly participate in climbing and secondary users who shape or support the climbing experience through learning, gym access, events, and community culture.

Primary Users

The primary users are student climbers, beginner climbers, and socially motivated climbers who need support with participation, improvement, and staying engaged with the climbing community.

These users benefit from a mobile-first system because they need low-friction access to information, lightweight progress tracking, and easy coordination with other climbers.

Secondary Users

Secondary users include regular indoor climbers, experienced peers, coaches, climbing gym staff, and event organisers who shape route access, learning opportunities, newcomer onboarding, and community culture.

They may not be the first-time beginner audience, but they can contribute content, organise activities, invite others, and help sustain the platform's social value.

Current pain points, playful requirements, and evidence of life

View journey map explanation
A. Current-State Journey Map

Scenario: A climber wants to keep improving, stay motivated, discover climbing opportunities, and feel connected to a climbing community.

B. Explanatory Paragraph

This journey map shows that the central problem is not limited to the climbing session itself. Instead, the experience breaks down across the transitions before, during, and between sessions. Users may enjoy climbing, yet still struggle to sustain participation because progress is hard to see, social opportunities are hard to discover, and motivation fades once the session ends. The most important themes revealed here are therefore motivation, progress visibility, and social connectedness. A successful system should not simply digitise logging; it should connect these moments into a more continuous experience.

C. Journey-to-Design Link

The journey map directly informs the next-stage requirements. Pain points around fragmented planning suggest the need for partner-finding and event discovery. Pain points during and after climbing suggest the need for low-friction logging and clear progress summaries. Pain points between sessions suggest the need for playful motivational loops, such as milestones, streaks, or friendly challenges. Together, these opportunities create a strong bridge from journey analysis to concrete user, functional, and playful requirements.

Requirements List

Three must-haves for a playful system

The system is considered playful only if it supports progress, social motivation, and discovery without turning the experience into pressure-based competition.

  • Session-to-Session Progress Quest: Users receive small achievable milestones that make gradual improvement visible between climbing sessions.
  • Climb Circles and Friendly Micro-Challenges: Users can join small-group challenges that support social motivation without public leaderboard pressure.
  • Exploration and Community Discovery Badges: Users unlock lightweight badges for attending events, joining beginner sessions, climbing with new partners, or trying new wall areas.
View supporting requirement details
Requirement Derivation

Users need continuity before, during, and after climbing

  • Users need a simple way to see their climbing progress over time.
  • Users need to remember routes, projects, and recent sessions without heavy manual effort.
  • Users need easier ways to find climbing partners, social sessions, and relevant events.
  • Beginner users need a product that feels welcoming, low-pressure, and confidence-building.
  • Regular users need features that help them maintain momentum between sessions.
  • Users need playful experiences that feel motivating and meaningful, rather than purely competitive.
  • Users need a mobile experience that fits naturally into before-, during-, and after-session use.
Research Insights Summary

Clarity, continuity, and low-pressure connection

The findings suggest that an effective climbing community app should prioritise clarity, continuity, and social accessibility over feature quantity.

  • Progress needs to be visible and meaningful when improvement is gradual.
  • Social connectedness supports retention, but should be practical and low-pressure.
  • Beginners need reassurance, while regular climbers need continuity across sessions.
  • Playful design works best when it encourages motivation without unnecessary pressure.
Functional Requirements

What the system shall support

  1. The system shall allow users to log climbs or sessions quickly on mobile.
  2. The system shall provide a personal progress view showing recent activity, milestones, and trends.
  3. The system shall allow users to save projects, notes, or route memories for later sessions.
  4. The system shall provide partner-finding or session coordination features.
  5. The system shall display events, beginner-friendly sessions, or community opportunities.
  6. The system shall support optional challenge participation, rather than forcing all users into competitive modes.
UX Requirements

How the experience should feel

  • The interface should support low-friction input so that logging a session takes minimal time.
  • The system should present progress in a clear and encouraging way, avoiding information overload.
  • The product should feel inclusive and beginner-friendly, not optimised only for advanced climbers.
  • Social features should be opt-in and privacy-aware, with small-group or friend-based interaction preferred over forced public exposure.
  • Playful elements should remain supportive rather than pressuring, especially for early-stage users.
Prioritised List

Must, should, and could priorities

Must: quick logging, progress dashboard, saved projects, partner-finding, event discovery, progress quests, climb circles, discovery badges, beginner-friendly UX.

Should: achievement sharing, beta sharing, optional reminders, personalised suggestions, beginner onboarding pathways.

Could: seasonal campaigns, coach tools, gym exploration maps, deeper analytics, wearable or sensor integration later.

Evidence of Life

5 interview photos

Interview Requirement Insights

Five recurring requirement themes

  1. Progress visibility: Users wanted a clearer way to see whether they were improving over time, suggesting a progress dashboard with recent sessions, milestones, and personal improvement.
  2. Session and route memory: Users found it difficult to remember routes, projects, and notes from previous sessions, suggesting quick session logging and saved project records.
  3. Social coordination: Beginners and socially motivated climbers wanted easier ways to find climbing partners, join small groups, or discover beginner-friendly sessions.
  4. Supportive playfulness: Users were interested in badges, quests, and friendly challenges, but preferred them to be encouraging rather than competitive or pressure-based.
  5. Beginner-friendly experience: Beginner users needed a welcoming, simple, and non-intimidating interface with clear wording, beginner tags, low-pressure interaction, and positive feedback.

What We Learned from Crazy Eights

Progress, community, and discovery formed the strongest direction

From the Crazy Eights sketches, we found that the most useful ideas were the ones that made progress easy to see, supported small-group social interaction, and encouraged users to discover new climbing opportunities. These directions matched our earlier research findings, especially the need for clearer session-to-session continuity, easier partner-finding, and playful but low-pressure motivation.

The sketching exercise also helped us narrow the scope for the next stage. Some ideas, such as quick logging, circles, challenges, and event discovery, were easier to turn into screens and user flows. Other ideas, such as a large public feed or more detailed analytics, felt less suitable for an initial prototype. Based on this, our low-fidelity prototype should focus on a hybrid direction that combines progress, community, and discovery in a simpler mobile-first flow.

Alternative 1

Social Matching First

Concept: The system is primarily organised around helping users find climbing partners, join sessions, and stay socially connected, with progress tracking as a supporting feature.

View evaluation details

Core interaction model: Users enter the app to discover who to climb with, what sessions are available, and what events are happening nearby. Social coordination is the main entry point.

Strengths

  • Directly addresses partner-finding and community discovery pain points
  • Strong for beginners who need low-pressure entry into the community
  • Creates visible social value quickly

Weaknesses

  • Progress tracking may feel secondary
  • Could become too close to a coordination tool rather than a climbing-growth product
  • Risk of weak long-term value if users do not use social features often

Alternative 2

Progress Tracking First

Concept: The system is primarily structured around logging climbs, remembering projects, and visualising improvement over time, with social features layered around that core.

View evaluation details

Core interaction model: Users come into the app mainly to log sessions, review progress, and receive supportive goal prompts or quests.

Strengths

  • Strong match for the clearest recurring need: visible and meaningful progress
  • Easy to justify personas and questionnaire findings
  • Gives the product a clear functional core

Weaknesses

  • Social/community value may feel less immediate
  • May appear too close to existing tracking tools if not designed carefully
  • Risks becoming individually useful but socially thin

Playfulness support: Supports Session-to-Session Progress Quest strongly, but needs careful integration of circles and discovery to avoid feeling too private.

Technical feasibility: High. Logging, dashboard views, saved projects, and milestones are relatively clear to prototype as a web app.

Suitable user type: Best for regular climbers and users who already climb consistently but want better continuity and motivation.

Alternative 3

Hybrid Progress + Community Motivation

Concept: The system combines a personal progress core with small-group social motivation and lightweight discovery features, balancing individual continuity with community participation.

View evaluation details

Core interaction model: Users can quickly log sessions and see progress, but the app also surfaces circles, beginner sessions, events, partner-finding, and exploration rewards in connected ways.

Strengths

  • Responds to all three strongest design opportunities: progress, motivation, and social connectedness
  • Fits the must-have playful features more naturally than the other two options
  • More differentiated from existing products because it combines multiple user needs coherently

Weaknesses

  • Requires tighter scope control to avoid feature overload
  • Needs careful homepage structure so that the system still feels simple
  • Must avoid becoming a weak compromise between too many directions

Playfulness support: Strongest overall fit for progress quests, micro-challenges, and discovery badges together.

Technical feasibility: Moderate to high if scoped carefully. A focused MVP can implement the core flow without building every social feature in full detail.

Suitable user type: Best for a mixed target group including beginners, student climbers, and regular users who want both progress and social motivation.

Final Choice

Hybrid progress with low-pressure community motivation

Based on the second-stage findings, the most suitable direction for the project is a hybrid model that combines progress tracking with low-pressure community motivation. The research does not support a purely social app or a purely individual tracking app. Instead, the findings repeatedly show that users want to keep improving, stay motivated between sessions, and feel socially connected without being pushed into overly competitive interaction. A hybrid direction therefore provides the best fit with the project's main pain points and must-have playful requirements.

In practice, this means the low-fidelity prototype should be built around a clear personal progress core, supported by small-group challenges, partner-finding, and community discovery. This direction is also realistic for implementation because the first prototype does not need to build every feature at full depth; it only needs to demonstrate a coherent user flow in which logging, progress feedback, and social motivation reinforce one another. For a student web-app project, this hybrid model offers the strongest balance between human-centred justification, playful interaction, and feasible development scope.

Iterative Refinement

Before and after changes based on user feedback

The alpha testing results directly shaped four interface refinements. Each change responded to a specific usability problem observed during testing.

Change 1

Bottom Navigation Optimization

Problem identified: Users felt the bottom navigation bar took up too much screen space.

Evidence: In the iteration record, users directly commented that the bottom navigation was too large and reduced available content space.

Improvement made: The bottom navigation was visually tightened so that it used less vertical space while still keeping the main navigation items accessible.

Why this was better: This improved mobile usability by allowing more page content to remain visible, reducing unnecessary scrolling and making the interface feel lighter.

Change 2

Card Layout and Information Density

Problem identified: Users felt that several card components had too much blank space and did not show enough useful information.

Evidence: The iteration record shows user feedback that cards were too spacious and occupied too much screen area.

Improvement made: Card spacing was reduced and the layout was adjusted to improve information density. Important content became easier to scan within the same screen area.

Why this was better: The revised layout made the app feel more efficient and better suited for repeated use, especially on mobile screens where space is limited.

Change 3

Rank Upgrade and Achievement Feedback

Problem identified: Users did not feel enough achievement or motivation when they upgraded rank or unlocked badges.

Evidence: The iteration record notes that users felt rank upgrades lacked celebration feedback, reducing the sense of accomplishment.

Improvement made: Rank upgrades and badge unlocks were connected to visible feedback, including notification messages and red-dot notification indicators. Users can now see achievement-related updates in the Notifications page.

Why this was better: This made the progress system feel more rewarding and helped reinforce the motivational purpose of RockBond.

Change 4

Community Partners Page Structure

Problem identified: Users found it unclear when existing friends and recommended partners were shown in the same list.

Evidence: The iteration record states that users wanted the friends page to distinguish between current friends and recommended community partners.

Improvement made: The Community Partners page was divided into two sections: My Friends and Recommended Partners. Existing friends now show a message action, while recommended partners show add or pending request states.

Why this was better: This made the social relationship status clearer and helped users understand what action they could take with each person.

Reflection on Iteration

From visually complete to more usable

The usability testing helped us move RockBond from a visually complete prototype toward a more usable and understandable web app. The main improvements focused on reducing layout friction, improving information clarity, and strengthening motivational feedback.

User feedback directly influenced changes to navigation, card layout, achievement notifications, and partner discovery. These refinements made the app more practical for the target users and better aligned with the original goal of helping climbers track progress, find partners, and stay motivated.

Final Reflection

Social, ethical, and AI-use reflection

This reflection summarises the final project outcome, the social and ethical implications of the design, and how generative AI was used responsibly during development.

Overall Outcome

A human-centred climbing community prototype

Overall, RockBond developed from a research-led idea into a mobile-first web prototype for everyday climbers. The final system focused on three connected values identified throughout our process: making climbing progress more visible, supporting low-pressure social motivation, and helping users discover climbing opportunities more easily.

The project outcome was not intended to be a full commercial climbing platform. Instead, it functioned as a functional human-centred prototype that demonstrated the core interaction logic of a playful climbing community system for beginner-to-intermediate climbers.

Social Implications

Supportive participation, not pressure

RockBond has the potential to make climbing participation more accessible and socially supportive. Beginner-friendly events, partner-finding, and climb circles may reduce the awkwardness of entering a new sports community, while progress quests and small-group challenges may help regular climbers stay motivated between sessions.

However, playful features could become harmful if implemented as high-pressure competition. Public comparison, rankings, or constant progress reminders could make beginners or less active climbers feel excluded. Therefore, RockBond should maintain a low-pressure social model where challenges are optional, feedback is encouraging, and users control how much they share.

Ethical Implications

Privacy, inclusion, and persuasive design

Research participation should remain informed, voluntary, and withdrawable. For the system itself, climbing logs, goals, routes, partner activity, and community participation can reveal personal routines, physical activity patterns, location preferences, and social relationships.

A future version should minimise unnecessary data collection, avoid exposing personal activity publicly by default, and give users control over what they share. It should also reward participation, confidence-building, exploration, and small improvements rather than only advanced grades or competitive rankings.

View limitations and future improvements
Limitations

What the project could not fully cover

  • The number of research and testing participants was limited, so findings may not represent all climbers.
  • The prototype mainly focused on beginner-to-intermediate and student climbers.
  • Advanced climbers, coaches, and gym operators were not fully represented.
  • Some social features were simplified and did not include full real-time matching, messaging, or event management.
  • The system demonstrates core interaction logic but does not yet include robust account security, long-term storage, or production-level privacy controls.
  • Evaluation was conducted on an alpha-level prototype, so it could test usability and concept clarity, but not long-term motivation or retention.
Future Improvements

How RockBond could develop further

  • Conduct a larger usability study with beginners, regular climbers, coaches, and gym staff.
  • Support richer route history, project notes, and personal goal customisation.
  • Add stronger privacy controls for sessions, achievements, and social activity visibility.
  • Test quests and badges over a longer period to ensure they motivate without creating pressure.
  • Extend community features with event creation, partner availability, and gym-based discovery.
Technical Reflection on AI Use

AI as development support, not design replacement

AI tools supported code planning, implementation, debugging, deployment guidance, and writing, while the project direction, user research, feature priorities, and final design decisions remained team-led.

AI Tools Used

ChatGPT / Codex and AI-assisted coding

We used ChatGPT / Codex for code planning, implementation support, debugging, deployment guidance, and writing. AI-assisted coding helped generate and refine React components, localStorage logic, UI interactions, and feature improvements.

AI image generation or image-assisted asset creation was also used where visual support was needed, such as prototype avatars or supporting visual assets.

Human Responsibility

What AI did not replace

AI did not replace the main design reasoning of the project. Topic selection, target-user identification, pain-point interpretation, usability testing, feature prioritisation, and evaluation of whether the prototype met course requirements were driven by the group.

AI was mainly used as a development and writing support tool, while the final product direction and design priorities were decided by the team.

Overall AI Reflection

Faster development still needed human judgement

Using AI made the development process faster and helped the team explore implementation alternatives more efficiently. It was especially useful for translating feature requirements into React code, debugging issues, and improving written documentation.

However, AI did not remove the need for human judgement. The team still had to decide which features mattered, test the prototype with real users, evaluate whether the output matched the project goals, and correct issues introduced during implementation. Overall, AI was most effective as a development assistant rather than a replacement for design thinking, user research, or final technical responsibility.

Ethical Considerations of AI Use

Accessibility, bias, privacy, and transparency

  • AI-generated UI needed review for labels, accessible names, readable contrast, and actions that were not dependent only on color.
  • Mock profiles and avatars should avoid implying that climbing communities include only one type of user.
  • AI-generated code required human review because it could miss project-specific constraints or runtime edge cases.
  • localStorage is suitable for a prototype but not secure enough for sensitive production data.
  • AI contribution should be clearly acknowledged in the portfolio and references.
